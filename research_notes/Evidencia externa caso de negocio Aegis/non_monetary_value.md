Reliability tags: [PRIMARY] = read from the publisher; [SECONDARY] = the publisher's figure as reported by press or summaries; [VENDOR] = company with a commercial interest; [OLD] = before 2018.

## 1. User trust and loyalty: how many users leave after errors or slowness?

### Takeaway
The evidence that users punish bad or slow digital experiences is consistent, but almost all of it comes from consultancies or vendors (PwC, Google, Deloitte, Akamai), and most of the speed figures are 2016–2020 e-commerce data. On a slide, these show the direction of the effect (users leave), not exact amounts for Aegis's setting. The most defensible single figure is PwC 2018: 32% of customers would leave a brand they love after one bad experience (n = 15,000, 12 countries). It is a stated intention in a survey, not observed behaviour.

### Cited Findings
- **PwC, "Experience is everything: Here's how to get it right" (Consumer Intelligence Series, 2018).** 32% of consumers say they would walk away from a brand they love after just one bad experience. Sample: 15,000 consumers in 12 countries (about 4,000 US and 11,000 from 11 other countries), online survey plus interviews. Latin America: 49%. Same survey: 73% say experience is a key driver of brand loyalty; people would pay a price premium of up to 16% for good experience. Reliability: [SECONDARY] (the PwC page returned 403; figures consistent across Adobe, NextTV and PwC regional PDFs). This is a self-reported intention to leave, not measured churn, and it covers brands in general, not IT outages specifically. Commercial consultancy. — [Adobe blog summary](https://blog.adobe.com/en/publish/2018/04/05/new-study-finds-consumers-would-pay-more-for-better-cx-pwc); [NextTV](https://www.nexttv.com/news/pwc-consumers-will-pay-more-better-experience-418882); [PwC CX placemat PDF](https://www.pwc.com/cb/en/services/consulting/pdf/customer-experience-placemat.pdf); original report URL (blocked to the fetcher): [PwC future of CX](https://www.pwc.com/us/en/services/consulting/library/consumer-intelligence-series/future-of-customer-experience.html)
- **Google / SOASTA, "The Need for Mobile Speed" (2016) and the "new industry benchmarks" article (Feb 2017).** 53% of mobile site visits are abandoned if a page takes longer than 3 seconds to load. Based on analysis of more than 10,000 mobile web domains (Google DoubleClick data). The average mobile landing page took 22 s to fully load (19 s on 3G). Sites loading in 5 s instead of 19 s had 25% higher ad viewability, 70% longer sessions and 35% lower bounce rates. Reliability: [VENDOR] Google has an interest in a faster web; [OLD] 2016 networks and devices; the method is not peer-reviewed. — [Think with Google, mobile page speed benchmarks](https://thinkwithgoogle.com/intl/en-gb/marketing-strategies/app-and-mobile/mobile-page-speed-new-industry-benchmarks); [Marketing Dive](https://www.marketingdive.com/news/google-53-of-mobile-users-abandon-sites-that-take-over-3-seconds-to-load/)
- **Google commissioned Fifty-five and Deloitte Digital, "Milliseconds Make Millions" (2020).** A 0.1 s improvement in four mobile speed metrics was associated with +8.4% conversions on retail sites and +10.1% on travel sites. Luxury shoppers were the most speed-sensitive. Data: about 30 million mobile sessions from 37 European and US brands (retail 20.5 M sessions, 15 brands; travel 7.4 M; luxury 2.1 M, 10 brands). Reliability: [VENDOR]-commissioned (Google); observational correlation, not a controlled experiment; e-commerce only. — [The Drum, 21 May 2020](https://www.thedrum.com/news/2020/05/21/milliseconds-make-millions-report-finds-faster-site-speed-increases-revenue-brands); [web.dev case study](https://web.dev/case-studies/milliseconds-make-millions)
- **Akamai, "State of Online Retail Performance" (Spring 2017).** A 100 ms delay in load time can hurt conversion rates by up to 7%. A 2 s delay increased bounce rates by up to 103% and cut session length by up to 51%. Data: one month, about 10 billion user visits to top retail sites (SOASTA mPulse RUM). Reliability: [VENDOR] (Akamai sells performance and CDN services); [OLD] 2017; quote these as "up to" figures. — [Akamai press release](https://www.akamai.com/fr/newsroom/press-release/akamai-releases-spring-2017-state-of-online-retail-performance-report); [eCommerceNews AU](https://ecommercenews.com.au/story/millisecond-delays-can-hurt-conversion-rates-online-transactions-akamai-report)

### Inferences
- For a "beyond money" slide, the honest framing is: "Users treat failures and slowness as broken trust. About one in three say they would leave a brand they love after one bad experience (PwC 2018, n = 15,000)." That supports the claim that fewer failed requests during spikes protects user trust and loyalty, which for public or non-commercial services means continued use and citizens' trust.
- The speed figures (53% at 3 s, 0.1 s → +8%) are about page load latency, not 5xx errors during autoscaling spikes. They are an analogy for "degraded service": they show that users are very sensitive, but they do not measure what Aegis changes.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed HCI study on users' tolerance of server errors (HTTP 5xx) or outages specifically, in the time available. Classic HCI response-time thresholds (Nielsen/Miller 0.1 s / 1 s / 10 s) exist but were not verified here and are [OLD].
- I could not open the PwC primary page (403), so the exact wording and question phrasing were not verified.
- There is no recent (2023–2026) independent replication of the 53% / 3 s figure.

## 2. Engineer toil and on-call burden

### Takeaway
Google's SRE book gives the conceptual anchor: toil is manual, reactive, automatable work; Google caps it at 50% of an SRE's time and measured about 33%; too much toil causes burnout and stalls careers. Industry reports (Catchpoint, PagerDuty, DORA) show that toil is still significant (median in the tens of percent), that off-hours incident work is linked to attrition, and that unstable, interrupt-driven work raises burnout. Fewer incidents, which proactive scaling aims at, means less interrupt-driven work and more capacity for engineering.

### Cited Findings
- **Google, "Site Reliability Engineering" book, ch. 5 "Eliminating Toil" (2016, free online).** Definition: toil is "the kind of work tied to running a production service that tends to be manual, repetitive, automatable, tactical, devoid of enduring value, and that scales linearly as a service grows". It is "interrupt-driven and reactive, rather than strategy-driven and proactive". Cap: "an advertised goal of keeping operational work (i.e., toil) below 50% of each SRE's time." Measured: "Quarterly surveys of Google's SREs show that the average time spent toiling is about 33%." Consequences: "Too much toil leads to burnout, boredom, and discontent"; career progress slows. Reliability: [PRIMARY], an authoritative and widely cited practitioner reference; [OLD] 2016, but still the standard definition. — [sre.google, Eliminating Toil](https://sre.google/sre-book/eliminating-toil/)
- **Catchpoint, "The SRE Report 2025" (Jan 2025; survey of SRE/DevOps practitioners, n ≈ 300+; exact n not verified).** Toil rose in 2024 for the first time in five years, after falling steadily since 2020. Sources disagree on the median: the report's own chart shows a median of 14% in 2024 (IQR 7–26%); ITPro Today says "median 20%, up from 14%"; Business Wire and APMdigest say "median 30% from 25%". Reliability: [VENDOR] (Catchpoint sells monitoring); the median is **unresolved**, so cite only "toil rose for the first time in five years". — [Catchpoint SRE Report 2025 PDF](https://resources.catchpoint.com/hubfs/Website%20Assets%20-%20Briefs,%20EBooks,%20etc/The%20SRE%20Report%202025%20Catchpoint.pdf); [Rootly summary](https://rootly.com/blog/sre-report-2025---key-takeaway); [ITPro Today](https://itprotoday.com/it-operations/site-reliability-engineering-teams-face-rising-challenges)
- **PagerDuty, "State of Digital Operations Report 2021"** (platform telemetry, Jan 2019–Apr 2021, PagerDuty customers). It found a statistically significant correlation between responders leaving the platform and how often they were involved in off-hours incident resolution. About one third of users had a much less consistent schedule in 2020 than in 2019, which PagerDuty equated to about 2 extra hours of work per day. Benchmark: about 2 interruptions per responder per month is "good", 7 is "bad", 19 is a "sign of burnout". Reliability: [VENDOR] (incident-management vendor) but based on real platform data; it is a correlation, and the attrition proxy is "left the platform". — [PagerDuty report PDF (mirror)](https://zporigin.leaddev.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/The-State-of-Digital-Operations-Report-2021.pdf); [PagerDuty takeaways blog](https://www.pagerduty.com/?p=70185)
- **PagerDuty survey (2022 coverage), n = 700 developers and IT operations professionals:** 3 in 5 work 10+ extra hours per week; 2 in 5 expect burnout to become an issue. Reliability: [VENDOR] survey, [SECONDARY] coverage. — [BetaNews, Jul 2022](https://betanews.com/2022/07/28/increased-expectations-see-burnout-on-the-rise-among-enterprise-tech-teams/)
- **DORA / Google Cloud, "Accelerate State of DevOps Report 2024"** (about 39,000 respondents over the program's history; about 3,000 in 2024, not verified here). Unstable organizational priorities lead to meaningful decreases in productivity and substantial increases in burnout. The report concludes that strong leadership, platforms or AI do not counteract this effect. Reliability: survey-based, large sample, independent research team (Google-funded); widely regarded as the most rigorous industry DevOps research. — [DORA 2024 report PDF](https://services.google.com/fh/files/misc/2024_final_dora_report.pdf); [Rootly summary for SREs](https://rootly.com/blog/dora-24-report-whats-in-it-for-sres)

### Inferences
- Bridge to Aegis: when the service degrades during spikes, responders are interrupted (pages, often off-hours), which is exactly what the SRE book calls toil and what PagerDuty links to attrition. Halving degraded time and cutting failed requests plausibly reduces pages. Aegis's tests did not measure pages or on-call load, so state this as expected, not measured.
- A slide line could be: "Every avoided incident is time engineers get back. Google caps toil at 50% because more causes burnout (Google SRE)."

### Gaps
- I found no rigorous study that converts "N fewer incidents" into "X engineer-hours regained". PagerDuty's "2 extra hours per day" is about schedule disruption during COVID, not per incident.
- The Catchpoint toil median is unresolved (14% vs 20% vs 30% depending on source).
- DORA 2023 reliability findings ("non-linear" returns of SRE investment) were not verified in detail.

## 3. Sustainability: energy and emissions from idle or overprovisioned servers

### Takeaway
Data centres are a large and fast-growing electricity user: about 415 TWh, about 1.5% of world electricity in 2024, roughly doubling to about 945 TWh by 2030 (IEA); 4.4% of US electricity in 2023 (LBNL). Servers waste energy when underused because an idle server still draws about half its peak power (Barroso & Hölzle). Vendor telemetry shows Kubernetes clusters typically use only about 8–20% of requested CPU. So right-sizing replicas, rather than overprovisioning "just in case", has a real but modest-per-cluster sustainability benefit.

### Cited Findings
- **IEA, "Energy and AI" (April 2025).** Data centres used about 415 TWh in 2024 (about 1.5% of global electricity), projected to reach about 945 TWh by 2030 (Base Case), more than doubling; AI is the main driver; the US accounts for almost half of the growth. A 2026 IEA update reportedly gives 485 TWh for 2025 (not verified on iea.org). Reliability: intergovernmental agency, high reliability. — [Scientific American](https://scientificamerican.com/article/ai-will-drive-doubling-of-data-center-energy-demand-by-2030); [India Environment Portal (report copy)](https://www.indiaenvironmentportal.org.in/reports-and-documents/energy-and-ai); primary at iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai (not fetched)
- **Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (Shehabi et al.), "2024 United States Data Center Energy Usage Report" (LBNL-2001637, Dec 2024, for US DOE).** US data centres used about 176 TWh in 2023 = 4.4% of US electricity (up from 58 TWh in 2014); projected 325–580 TWh by 2028 (6.7%–12% of US electricity). Reliability: national lab, peer-reviewed government report, high reliability. — [LBNL publication page](https://datacenters.lbl.gov/publications/2024-lbnl-data-center-energy-usage-report); [Berkeley Lab news](https://newscenter.lbl.gov/2025/01/15/berkeley-lab-report-evaluates-increase-in-electricity-demand-from-data-centers/)
- **Barroso & Hölzle, "The Case for Energy-Proportional Computing", IEEE Computer (2007).** Even an energy-efficient server "still consumes about half its full power when doing virtually no work", and servers usually run at low utilization, where they are least efficient. Related work: a server at 10% load draws 50–60% of peak power. Reliability: peer-reviewed and seminal; [OLD] 2007. Modern servers have lower idle power, but idle draw is still well above zero. — [Barroso & Hölzle 2007 PDF](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/courses/archive/spring13/cos598C/Barroso07_EnergyProp-clean.pdf); [Wikipedia overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_proportional_computing)
- **Koomey & Taylor (Anthesis Group / Stanford), "comatose servers" (June 2015).** 30% of about 4,000 physical servers (TSO Logic data) had done no useful work in over 6 months; extrapolated to about 10 million comatose servers worldwide. A 2017 follow-up ("Comatose Servers Redux", 16,000 servers) found about 25% of physical servers and about 30% of VMs comatose. Uptime Institute estimates are 20–30%; McKinsey (2008) said "up to 30%". Reliability: small, non-random samples; vendor data source; [OLD]. Koomey himself cautioned that more work was needed. — [Computerworld 2015](https://www.computerworld.com/article/1631910/1-in-3-data-center-servers-is-a-zombie.html); [Comatose Servers Redux PDF](https://info.anthesisgroup.com/hubfs/Website%20PDFs/Comatose-Servers-Redux.pdf); [NRDC, "The Case of the Zombie Servers"](https://www.nrdc.org/bio/pierre-delforge/case-zombie-servers)
- **NRDC (2014 Data Center Efficiency Assessment), via Computerworld:** US data centres used 91 billion kWh in 2013. Removing idle servers and other efficiency measures could cut use by about 40%. Reliability: NGO; [OLD]. — [Computerworld 2015](https://www.computerworld.com/article/1631910/1-in-3-data-center-servers-is-a-zombie.html)
- **Kubernetes utilization (vendor telemetry):**
  - CAST AI 2026 "State of Kubernetes Resource Optimization": average CPU utilization about 8% across production clusters, memory about 20%; 69% of clusters overprovision CPU. [VENDOR] (sells K8s optimization). — [daily.dev summary](https://app.daily.dev/posts/2026-state-of-kubernetes-resource-optimization-cpu-at-8-memory-at-20-and-getting-worse-hhv7ipuuy); [CAST AI blog](https://cast.ai/blog/why-are-kubernetes-costs-so-high/)
  - Sysdig (2023 Cloud-Native report): 69% of requested CPU was not used. [VENDOR]. — [Sysdig blog](https://sysdig.com/blog/millions-wasted-kubernetes)
  - Datadog (State of Containers / Cloud Costs): over-provisioning described as a main source of container waste; autoscaling is recommended. Secondary sources attribute "83% of container costs come from idle resources" and ">65% of containers use < half requested CPU" to Datadog 2025 (**not verified on datadoghq.com**). [VENDOR]. — [Datadog cloud-costs learnings](https://datadoghq.com/blog/cloud-costs-study-learnings); [FinOps Weekly](https://finopsweekly.com/blog/you-moved-to-kubernetes-your-cpu-utilization-is-still-10/)

### Inferences
- The chain for the slide: overprovisioned pods → nodes kept running at low utilization → servers at low load still draw about half their peak power → avoidable energy and emissions. More predictable resource use from Aegis lets operators run fewer standby replicas or nodes. Aegis's tests measured resource predictability, not kWh, so any energy claim should be qualitative or an explicitly labelled estimate.
- Strongest citable context: "Data centres: about 1.5% of world electricity (2024), doubling by 2030 (IEA); K8s clusters use only about 8–20% of requested CPU (industry telemetry)."

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed study that directly quantifies energy wasted by low Kubernetes CPU utilization (the K8s figures are all vendor telemetry).
- I did not locate a recent Uptime Institute figure for average server utilization (its 2023 IT efficiency survey mainly reports that fewer than one third of operators can measure work per energy). — [Uptime data report 123](https://intelligence.uptimeinstitute.com/sites/default/files/2024-01/Data%20report%20123_Tools%20to%20watch%20and%20improve%20power%20use%20by%20IT%20are%20underused.pdf)
- No emissions factor (gCO2/kWh) conversion was researched.

## 4. Trust in automated operations (explainability)

### Takeaway
The evidence that explainable automation is trusted more by operators is thin and mostly vendor surveys. They consistently report that black-box behaviour and false insights erode trust and that operators rate explainability as important. Academic work mainly lists explainability as an open challenge for AIOps rather than measuring its effect. Aegis's plain-language explanation of each decision addresses a recognised concern, but no strong quantitative evidence of its effect on adoption was found.

### Cited Findings
- **Enterprise Management Associates (EMA) survey, n = 250 IT professionals (AI-driven network management, about 2023).** 96% had seen AI/ML tools produce false or mistaken insights; more than half said explainable AI is "very important" for building trust. Reliability: analyst firm, not peer-reviewed; [SECONDARY]; exact year and question wording not verified. — [CIO/ARN coverage](https://d2bs8hqp6qvsw6.cloudfront.net/article/707478/how-it-pros-might-learn-believe-ai-driven-network-management/)
- **BigPanda "Future of Monitoring and AIOps" survey (2019):** "black-box" AI/ML was a top concern for IT Ops respondents (sample not verified). Reliability: [VENDOR], [SECONDARY]. — [BigPanda press release](https://www.bigpanda.io/press-release/bigpanda-announces-the-results-of-its-future-of-monitoring-and-aiops-survey/)
- **Remil et al. (2024), "AIOps Solutions for Incident Management: Technical Guidelines and A Comprehensive Literature Review" (arXiv 2404.01363).** Lists interpretability, and the consistency and stability of explanations from AIOps models, as open research challenges. Reliability: academic literature review (preprint). — [EmergentMind summary of the open problem](https://api.emergentmind.com/open-problems/consistency-stability-aiops-model-interpretations); arXiv: https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.01363
- **"Too Much to Trust? Measuring the Security and Cognitive Impacts of Explainability in AI-Driven SOCs" (arXiv 2503.02065, 2025).** Surveys security analysts and incident responders. Explainability is expected to support analyst trust, but explanations that are too much or poorly designed add cognitive load. This is a security operations setting, an adjacent domain to IT operations. Reliability: academic preprint. — [arXiv 2503.02065v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.02065v2)

### Inferences
- A defensible slide claim: "Operators distrust black-box automation, and explainability is consistently rated a key condition for trust (EMA, n = 250). Aegis explains each scaling decision in plain words." Avoid claiming a measured increase in adoption.
- The SOC paper suggests a nuance worth keeping: short, plain explanations (Aegis's approach) are preferable to dumping model internals.

### Gaps
- I found no Gartner or Forrester public figure on AIOps trust (likely paywalled) and no peer-reviewed controlled study showing that explanations increase operator acceptance of autoscaling or AIOps decisions specifically.
- The broader human-automation trust literature (e.g., Lee & See 2004, "Trust in automation") is a seminal [OLD] reference that was not fetched or verified here.

## 5. Compliance, SLA and reputation (incl. non-commercial organisations)

### Takeaway
For EU financial entities, operational resilience is now a legal obligation: the Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA, Regulation (EU) 2022/2554, applicable since 17 January 2025) requires ICT risk management, business continuity and the reporting of major ICT incidents to supervisors, with penalties set by Member States. Reliability therefore carries regulatory and reputational weight beyond revenue. Evidence for other public-sector service standards was not researched in depth.

### Cited Findings
- **Regulation (EU) 2022/2554 (DORA).** Article 19 requires financial entities to report major ICT-related incidents to the competent authority in three stages: initial notification, intermediate report, and final report. The intermediate report is due within 72 hours of the initial notification (per the Austrian FMA). Articles 50–52: Member States must empower authorities to impose administrative penalties and remedial measures (the regulation does not set fixed fine amounts for financial entities) and may add criminal penalties. Reliability: EU law, highest reliability. The details here come from national supervisors and secondary summaries because the EUR-Lex fetch returned an empty page; **verify the article wording on EUR-Lex before citing**. — [EUR-Lex, Regulation (EU) 2022/2554](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2022/2554/oj/eng); [Austrian FMA, DORA ICT incidents](https://www.fma.gv.at/en/dora-ict-related-incidents/); [Deutsche Bundesbank, DORA](https://www.bundesbank.de/en/service/reporting-systems/banking-supervision/digital-operational-resilience-act-dora--968534); [Legiscope, DORA penalties](https://www.legiscope.com/blog/dora-penalties-enforcement.html)
- Unverified claim to avoid: a vendor page (FluxForce) states DORA fines of "2% of global annual turnover". I found no support for this in the regulation summaries. **Do not cite it.** — [FluxForce](https://www.fluxforce.ai/answers/what-is-dora)
- Not verified in this pass: DORA's date of application (17 Jan 2025, Art. 64) and the count of entity types in scope (Art. 2). Both come from background knowledge and should be checked on EUR-Lex.

### Inferences
- For a university or public-sector audience: even where DORA does not apply, the slide can frame reliability as accountability. Regulators (DORA), contracts (SLAs) and citizens (trust in public services) increasingly expect demonstrable resilience and incident transparency. Aegis's plain-language decision log supports post-incident reporting and audit, which is a natural link to DORA-style "explain what happened" duties. This is an inference, not a cited finding.

### Gaps
- EUR-Lex text not retrieved; article wording, the date of application and the scope of entities need verification.
- NIS2 (Directive (EU) 2022/2555), which covers public administration and digital infrastructure, was not researched but is likely relevant for non-financial and public organisations.
- Public-sector service standards (e.g., UK GDS Service Standard, Colombian/Latin American e-government availability norms) were not researched.
- No quantitative evidence was found on the reputational damage of outages for non-commercial organisations.
